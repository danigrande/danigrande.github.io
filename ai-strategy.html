The AI Product Strategy Framework

A six-module method that de-risks an AI product before a line of code is written — forcing the hard questions first and answering them with numbers, not adjectives.

Modules: 6 Type: Reusable method Applied to: Agente Mundial (Worldcup Pool)

The Bet The Moat The Margin The Contract The Guardrails The Pitch

Most AI products start with the technology and hope the business follows. This framework reverses that order. It front-loads the questions that are hardest to answer — and most expensive to get wrong — so that by the time anything gets built, the bet, the defensibility, and the economics are already on paper.

I built it as a sequence of six modules, each adding one layer of the case. Each module ends in a written artifact, and every claim in those artifacts carries a number: a score, a target, or a kill criterion.

I've applied the framework end-to-end to one product — Agente Mundial, the World Cup pool chatbot — so the method is shown working, not just described.

1 The Bet — What, For Whom, Why Now

The opening question is not "what can we build?" but "why will this win — and what would make us stop?" The Bet forces two things before anything else:

  • A three-axis vulnerability diagnostic — the idea is scored on its contextual moat, its data advantage, and its platform exposure (each out of 5), with a named attacker for each axis.
  • Kill criteria — the concrete evidence of failure, agreed up front. A product should know what failure looks like before it starts.

Artifacts: vulnerability diagnostic · prototype bet (AI value archetype) · kill criteria.

2 The Moat — Why It Won't Be Copied in Six Months

An AI feature is easy to clone. Defensibility has to come from something that compounds. The Moat scores four flywheel loops — correction, preference, domain context, and network — out of 20, then assumes the weakest loop is where a competitor attacks first.

  • Encroachment assessment — three attacker types (platform, vertical competitor, adjacent expansion), each with a vector and a time-to-threat.
  • Kill-switch audit — vendor dependency scored on provider, abstraction, routing, and evals, with a portability verdict: Ready / Partial / Locked.

Artifacts: data flywheel map · encroachment threat assessment · kill-switch audit.

3 The Margin — Make Money or Bleed It

Frontier-model quality on everything is a margin killer. The Margin builds the unit economics before the product does, then stress-tests them.

  • Cost model — inference, infrastructure, data, and human-in-the-loop, per user per month.
  • Cascading strategy — a cheap model for the volume and a frontier model for the judgment, with a routing rule and an expected cascade ratio.
  • Stress tests — what happens if inference costs 3x, the heaviest segment doubles, or the provider raises prices 50%.

Artifacts: cost curve · cascading strategy · pricing model · board one-pager.

4 The Contract — Why Users Trust a Probabilistic System

Users tolerate AI that is occasionally wrong — if it tells them when it isn't sure. The Contract turns vague quality claims into measurable promises.

  • Golden dataset — a fixed set of test cases (including adversarial ones) that define "good" in advance.
  • Reliability targets — accuracy, hallucination rate, p95 latency, and drift velocity, each with a measurement and an alert threshold.
  • Confidence UX — tiered signalling (high: no indicator; medium: a subtle badge; low: the system qualifies its answer).
  • Human-in-the-loop architecture — the explicit escalation path for everything the system isn't sure about.

Artifacts: golden dataset spec · reliability contract · confidence UX design · HITL architecture · red-team findings.

5 The Guardrails — What Breaks at Scale, What Compounds

Scale turns small gaps into systemic failures. The Guardrails audit the compounding loops and the boundaries of autonomy — what the system can do alone, what needs a human, and what it must never do.

  • Feedback-loop audit — every loop labelled active, broken, or missing, with a fix plan for the broken ones.
  • Agent topology — autonomy boundaries, escalation triggers, and an audit cadence.
  • Shadow AI audit — the workarounds users are already running themselves, triaged into keep / govern / kill.
  • Regulatory exposure — EU AI Act, GDPR, and COPPA mapped against the product.

Artifacts: compounding-system design · governance posture · shadow AI audit.

6 The Pitch — Fund It, Ship It, Grow It

The final module forces the case into a single narrative: a three-horizon roadmap, a one-sentence board thesis, the specific ask, and — critically — a comparison of where the strategy started versus where it is now.

  • Three horizons — Now (quick wins), Next (bets), and Bet (moonshots), each with a metric and a confidence level.
  • Board narrative — the one-sentence thesis, the case, the risks, and the ask.
  • Baseline vs. now — the M1 strategy written down verbatim, next to what it became. Strategy is a living thing.

Artifacts: three-horizon roadmap · board pitch · baseline-vs-now check.

7 The Principles That Make It Work

  • Numbers, not adjectives. "Strong moat" is an opinion; "2/5 data advantage" is a claim that can be argued with.
  • Red-team before you build. A partner plays the attacker and targets your weakest loop. Better to hear the attack from a friend than a competitor.
  • Kill criteria are a feature. Naming failure up front is what keeps a bet honest — not a concession of weakness.
  • Cascade the economics. Cheapest capable model for volume, frontier model for judgment. The ratio is a product decision, not an afterthought.
  • Baseline vs. now. The strategy is version-controlled and re-scored as assumptions change — the final module shows how much moved.
