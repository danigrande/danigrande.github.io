AI Strategy: Agente Mundial

The six-module strategy framework applied end-to-end to a real product — the World Cup pool chatbot — with every question answered in numbers, not adjectives.

Applied to: Agente Mundial (Worldcup Pool) Modules: 6 Status: Complete

This is the worked example behind the strategy framework. I built it for my own product — Agente Mundial, the World Cup pool chatbot — and answered every question with a number: a score, a target, or a kill criterion.

1 The Bet — What We're Building, For Whom, Why Now

A gamified, multi-personality chatbot for World Cup prediction pools. The AI value archetype is a Copilot — it doesn't decide anything. It entertains, informs, and keeps the group in the game.

2/5

4/5

Top risks: post-event churn (critical — the tournament ends), no data on day one (high), self-hosted infrastructure (medium), untested revenue model (medium), and LLM unit economics (mitigated by the cascading strategy).

Kill criteria: if the financial loss exceeds €2,000 with no evidence of revenue growth, the project stops. A product should know what failure looks like before it starts.

2 The Moat — Why It Won't Get Copied in Six Months

A chatbot is easy to clone. The defensibility comes from what compounds: a golden dataset of quality interactions, personalities that are culturally specific, and a scoring engine that is fiddly to rebuild.

10/20
Data flywheel score
85%
Vendor portability

Platform risk — Meta / Apple

Native AI agents could appear inside the OS or default group chats. Defense: a dedicated UI and culturally bold personalities that brand-safe horizontal platforms can't ship.

Vertical risk — Sleeper / ESPN

Existing fantasy-sports incumbents could expand. Defense: a different audience — Spanish-speaking football fans in WhatsApp groups — served by culture-specific anchors they'd have to rebuild from scratch.

Distribution risk — Telegram clones

An indie developer could ship the same idea as a Telegram mini-app. Defense: the scoring engine, transcreation pipeline, and seven personalities are months of work — and the dataset compounds meanwhile.

3 The Margin — Will It Make Money or Bleed It

Free product today; premium entertainment tomorrow. The math works because inference is cascaded: a self-hosted small model handles ~96% of traffic, and a frontier model handles the ~4% that is asynchronous.

~5,400
Paid groups to break even

Pricing: a $4.99 group pass per tournament (admin-sponsored) plus banner ads. At 10% conversion, the blended margin lands between 55–65%. Break-even needs ~5,400 paid groups; the monthly operating cost is covered by just 180–340 paid groups.

Cascading strategy: today, 100% of traffic runs on a 70B model's free tier. Tomorrow, a self-hosted 8B model handles nearly everything, with a small premium model for daily summaries.

4 The Contract — Why Users Trust a Probabilistic System

Fun product, serious promises. The contract sets measurable targets instead of vague quality claims:

92%
Accuracy target
<1%
Hallucination rate target
<800ms
p95 response latency

The golden dataset: 101 test cases across six categories — intent classification, personality responses, language purity, transcreation, edge cases, and daily summaries — including adversarial cases.

Confidence UX (designed): above 90% confidence, no indicator; 70–90%, a subtle badge; below 70%, the bot qualifies its answer ("No estoy seguro, pero…").

Human review triggers: downvotes, calibration signals, and responses that failed the quality gate three times. Daily review cap of 20, with deduplication rules.

Honest gaps: the golden dataset is still small for statistical significance, there is no latency enforcement, and no automated rollback. Naming the gaps is part of the contract.

5 The Guardrails — What Breaks at Scale

The quality gate compounds: failed responses retry up to three times with judge feedback injected into the prompt. Users never reach the model directly.

Autonomy boundaries

Solo: standard replies, web search, translations, summaries, corrections. Needs a human: forced approvals, downvotes, user reports. Never automatic: account deletion, impersonation.

Escalation

Judge failure → retry (max 3) → human review. Downvote → human review. User report → pending record. Rate limits guard the model itself.

Regulatory exposure

EU AI Act — minimal (entertainment). GDPR — limited data, but gaps: no lawful-basis register, incomplete deletion. COPPA — high exposure: no age gate yet.

Shadow AI audit: I also mapped the workarounds users were already doing on their own — seven hacks worth about $85/month, from what-if simulations to extra languages. Four became roadmap items; one was governed with rate limits.

Audit cadence: weekly golden-set evaluation, daily human review, quarterly policy review.

6 The Pitch — Fund It, Ship It, Grow It

Three horizons: Now — per-personality quality thresholds, leaderboard history, more direct-generation languages, match reminders. Next — what-if simulation, a prediction-strategy mode, shareable leaderboard cards. Bet — generic leagues (La Liga, Premier League), custom personalities, cross-group rankings.

The ask: $42K — $27K capital for monetization infrastructure plus $15K for three months of self-hosted GPUs. The board narrative: personality-driven commentary turns a static pool into a daily social ritual — and the 50-day tournament generates the data to validate a year-round product.

The most important decision: fine-tuning was dropped from all horizons. The data flywheel score was re-scored from 5/5 to 2/5 — fine-tuning improves the next event, not this one. Focus beats ambition.
