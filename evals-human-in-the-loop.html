Evals & Human in the Loop

How I keep AI output trustworthy at scale — measuring quality automatically, and routing the hard cases to a human.

Applied to: Agente Mundial (live) GrandeSuite Next (in development) Status: In production / in practice

LLM-as-a-Judge Golden Dataset Human Review Quality Gate

AI products fail quietly: a response is plausible but wrong, a personality drifts, a document is misread. You can't eyeball quality at scale — and you can't fix what you don't measure. So every AI output gets scored, and anything suspicious goes to a human.

1 The Two Use Cases

This discipline is applied in two very different products. Here is each in plain terms — and the problem evals solve for it.

Agente Mundial — a chatbot that talks to people

What it is: a chatbot inside a World Cup prediction-pool app. AI personalities banter with your friend group in 14 languages.

The problem: an AI that talks to people can sound plausible but be wrong, drift out of character, or slip into the wrong language — and one bad message costs trust. You can't read every message by hand.

How it's checked: a second, smaller AI scores every reply for quality and language purity; anything suspicious is flagged for a human.

GrandeSuite Next — an ERP where AI does the busywork

What it is: business software for growing companies, where AI reads supplier invoices, suggests which bills to pay, and helps with setup.

The problem: here the AI touches money. A misread number or a bad suggestion is expensive — so it has to be accurate and auditable, not just plausible.

How it's checked: per-field confidence scores flag uncertain data; anything below the bar goes to a human queue. Matching and payments stay rules-based — and the software itself is built by AI under tests and reviews.

Both are the same pattern: AI produces output, an automatic check scores it, and the hard cases go to a human.

2 Why Evals

  • Plausible-but-wrong is the default failure mode of language models — it reads fine and costs trust.
  • Consumer products can't ship unmeasured AI. One bad response can damage the brand; at scale, bad responses are guaranteed unless you filter them.
  • Evals are the test suite of AI — the same discipline as software tests, applied to probabilistic output.
  • What gets measured can be improved: scores feed prompt calibration and grow the golden dataset.

3 LLM-as-a-Judge

In Agente Mundial, a smaller, faster model evaluates chatbot responses in the same pipeline as generation. It scores two axes — language purity (0–10) and quality (0–10) — and every score is logged with the response's latency and tokens.

  • The judge model is a smaller LLM (llama-3.1-8b-instant) — cheap enough to run on everything.
  • A rule-based fast-path catches script contamination first: if a Spanish response is mixed with Hangul, Arabic, or Cyrillic, a regex check fails it at 0/10 without even calling the model.
  • Judging is sampleable — an EVAL_SAMPLE_RATE controls how much traffic is judged (default 100%), so the quality gate can be throttled under load.
  • The quality gate retries: a failed response regenerates up to three times, with the judge's feedback injected into the next attempt. After three failures it is "force-approved" and flagged for human review — never silently dropped, never silently shipped.

The targets the contract commits to:

92%
Accuracy target
<1%
Hallucination target
<800ms
p95 latency target

The golden dataset: 101 test cases across six categories — intent classification, personality responses, language purity, transcreation, edge cases, and daily summaries — including five adversarial cases built to break the system. The eval runner scores against it and persists pass rates per dataset, so regressions are visible run over run.

Calibration is the hard part. The judge and human reviewers disagreed on borderline responses. A structured rubric with examples improved alignment — but it remains an ongoing calibration problem, not a solved one. These targets are a contract to meet, not a score already achieved: the measurement infrastructure exists, but automated alerting and rollback are still missing.

4 Human in the Loop

Humans don't review everything — they review the right things. Human reviews are created automatically when:

  • A user downvotes (rating 1–2) — the response goes to review.
  • A calibration signal fires — a high user rating (4–5) that the judge disagreed with.
  • The quality gate exhausts its retries — a response force-approved after three failed attempts.

Guardrails on the review itself: a daily cap of 20 reviews, deduplication (max 3 similar reviews per personality + language per week), and a reviewer dashboard. A verdict can pass or fail with a confidence score and notes, and a passing case can be promoted into the golden dataset so the system gets permanently better.

Users also teach the bot directly: when someone corrects it, the correction is detected, classified, and logged into the same review pipeline — feedback becomes signal instead of noise.

5 Evals in Development

The same discipline applies to building the ERP with AI agents:

  • Readiness checks before code — plan, architecture, and design validated against each other before implementation.
  • Three independent reviews per change — a blind reviewer (bugs and security), an edge-case reviewer (every branch and boundary), and an acceptance reviewer (against the spec).
  • ~790 automated tests — tests are written before implementation, and every acceptance criterion maps to at least one test.

6 Confidence & Lessons

Confidence UX (designed, not yet shown to users): above 90% confidence, no indicator; 70–90%, a subtle badge; below 70%, the bot qualifies its answer ("No estoy seguro, pero…"). The judge scores exist and are visible in the developer dashboard, but the user-facing signal is one of the honest gaps still open.

What I'd do differently: start the quality pipeline on day one. Retrofitting the judge and review queue after the chatbot was already in use cost more than building it in from the start.

Documented gaps, not hidden ones: no on-call rotation, no automated retraining, and no automated rollback yet.
