AI Quality

Evals & Human in the Loop

How I keep AI output trustworthy at scale — measuring quality automatically, and routing the hard cases to a human.

Applied to: Agente Mundial (live) GrandeSuite Next (in development) Status: In production / in practice

AI products fail quietly: a response is plausible but wrong, a personality drifts, a document is misread. You can't eyeball quality at scale — and you can't fix what you don't measure.

So every AI output gets scored, and anything suspicious goes to a human. The same principle applies to building with AI: reviews and tests gate every change.

  • Plausible-but-wrong is the default failure mode of language models — it reads fine and costs trust.
  • Consumer products can't ship unmeasured AI. One bad response can damage the brand; at scale, bad responses are guaranteed unless you filter them.
  • Evals are the test suite of AI — the same discipline as software tests, applied to probabilistic output.
  • What gets measured can be improved: scores feed prompt calibration and grow the golden dataset.

Every chatbot response is evaluated by a smaller, faster model against relevance, tone, and factuality — automatically, in the same pipeline as the response itself.

The golden dataset: 101 test cases across six categories — intent classification, personality responses, language purity, transcreation, edge cases, and daily summaries — including five adversarial cases built to break the system.

Calibration is the hard part. The judge and human reviewers disagreed on borderline responses. A structured rubric with examples improved alignment — but it remains an ongoing calibration problem, not a solved one.

Humans don't review everything — they review the right things. The triggers:

  • Downvotes from users route the response to review.
  • Calibration signals — a high user rating that the judge scored as a failure.
  • Quality-gate exhaustion — responses that failed three automatic retries.

Guardrails on the review itself: a daily cap of 20 reviews, deduplication rules, and a reviewer dashboard. Corrections from users are detected, logged, and enter the same queue.

Users can also teach the bot directly: when someone corrects it, the correction is classified and saved for review — feedback becomes signal instead of noise.

The same discipline applies to building the ERP with AI agents:

  • Readiness checks before code — plan, architecture, and design validated against each other.
  • Three independent reviews per change — a blind reviewer (bugs and security), an edge-case reviewer (every branch and boundary), and an acceptance reviewer (against the spec).
  • ~790 automated tests — tests are written before implementation, and every acceptance criterion maps to at least one test.

Confidence UX (designed): above 90% confidence, no indicator; 70–90%, a subtle badge; below 70%, the bot qualifies its answer ("No estoy seguro, pero…"). Status: designed but not yet shown to users — one of the honest gaps.

What I'd do differently: start the quality pipeline on day one. Retrofitting the judge and review queue after the chatbot was already in use cost more than building it in from the start.

Documented gaps, not hidden ones: no on-call rotation, no automated retraining, and no automated rollback yet.
