Can one product manager, working with AI agents, take a real business problem all the way to working software? That is the experiment behind GrandeSuite Next — an entry-level ERP for growing businesses: buy, sell, get paid, pay suppliers, and keep the books, all in one system, designed and built end-to-end by one person with AI agents.

The case study below tells the whole story in five parts: the product strategy, the product architecture, the AI, the technical implementation, and what I'd do differently.

Part 1 · Product Strategy

The bet, the market, and the vision

Where the idea came from, who it is for, and why it should exist.

1 Why This Project

For six years I was a Principal Product Manager at Oracle NetSuite, working on the ERP. We wanted to build "NetSuite Next" — a new, smaller, modern version of the product. More than a thousand people worked on it, and after five years it still hadn't seen the light of day.

That experience stuck with me. What if the bottleneck wasn't the vision or the demand, but the sheer weight of shipping something that big? I wanted to know how far one person — using AI agents — could take a proof of concept for exactly that kind of product.

GrandeSuite Next is that experiment: an entry-level ERP for growing businesses, designed and built end-to-end by one product manager working with AI. Not to prove a point about AI, but to find out how much of a "thousand-person, five-year" project is actually reachable — and where the real hard parts still are.

2 The Problem

Growing businesses eventually outgrow the accounting software that got them started. The tools that work at $2M in revenue start to break at $20M. And the jump to enterprise software is brutal.

Five things go wrong again and again:

  • Invoices arrive everywhere. Paper, PDFs, emails, supplier portals — often in different inboxes. People retype them by hand, and errors slip in.
  • Prices creep up unnoticed. Nobody has time to check every invoice line against what was ordered — so supplier price increases surface weeks later, after the margin is already lost.
  • Approvals get stuck. Managers are on the floor or on the road, not at a desk signing paperwork. Payment terms slip.
  • Cash leaks quietly. Duplicate payments, missed supplier credits, and paying for short deliveries drain money without anyone noticing.
  • No separation of duties. The same person who approves a payment often releases it — with no audit trail of who authorized what.

The result: businesses lose 1–3% of their spend to invoice errors and spend 10–20 hours a month on manual workarounds. The alternative — enterprise software like NetSuite — means $150K+ quotes and 6–9 month implementations. As the pitch goes: QuickBooks stops scaling. NetSuite is terrifying.

3 Market & Opportunity

Who it is for. Businesses that have outgrown QuickBooks — typically 20–200 employees and $2–25M in revenue. Especially operations-heavy businesses like distributors, where inventory and paperwork are the daily reality.

Why now. There is a wave of "QuickBooks leavers." More than half of migrations start with a pricing shock, and most businesses know they need to move but delay 6–18 months — mostly out of fear that a new system will fail to get off the ground.

The competition. NetSuite is the default "safe choice," but it costs $150K+ with months of consultants. Sage Intacct and Microsoft Dynamics sit nearby. Intuit launched its own Enterprise Suite in 2026, aimed at the same buyer.

The gap. Nobody offers the operational core — purchasing, inventory, sales, and accounting — in one simple system, at a mid-market price, with a fast, fixed implementation.

The bet. A 5-day fixed-price onboarding ("Pit Bay") for $10K, then $500/month with frontline workers free. Year-one cost of $16–33K, compared with $50–100K+ for Microsoft Dynamics.

The wedge is operations-first: warehouse workers scan barcodes, the books stay current by themselves, and finance gets clean numbers without chasing spreadsheets. Implementation in days, not months.

4 Discovery & Research

Brainstorming shaped the concept: the North Star, the target market, and four pillars — fast onboarding templates, an event-driven accounting engine, a finance dashboard, and an AI copilot.

Domain research mapped every type of financial transaction in a business — from issuing a purchase order to settling a customer invoice — to its accounting impact. A second deep dive covered supplier invoice workflows, document-reading technology, and compliance requirements.

Market research studied "QuickBooks leavers": who they are, what triggers the switch, how they buy, and how NetSuite, Sage Intacct, and Xero price and position themselves. The pattern was clear: fear of implementation failure is the biggest barrier, and transparent pricing is the biggest opening.

Key insight that shaped the strategy: rather than charging per module like traditional ERP vendors, win on transparent pricing, a fast 5-day go-live, and free frontline users. Compete as operations-first, not another finance-first suite.

5 Product Vision

GrandeSuite Next is one place to run the financial life of a growing business: buy, sell, get paid, pay suppliers, and keep the books — no exports, no spreadsheets, no separate accounting package. The vision is a native general ledger as the single source of truth, with multi-entity and multi-currency support, and a QuickBooks bridge as an onboarding convenience for businesses transitioning off QBO — never a dependency.

Two everyday flows meet in one system:

  • Buying (procure-to-pay): order from suppliers → receive goods, scanned at the warehouse → get the invoice → pay.
  • Selling (order-to-cash): quote a customer → take the order → pick, pack, and ship → invoice → collect payment → handle returns.

Every step becomes bookkeeping automatically. The books are always current, and every number can be traced back to the real-world action that created it.

North Star: eliminate manual accounting by capturing what actually happens and turning it into double-entry bookkeeping automatically.

Honest current state: the MVP core is built and tested, and much of the phase-2 foundation (multi-entity, multi-currency, tax, revenue recognition, custom workflows, payroll) is in place. What remains is fast-follow scope and documentation — see Progress.

6 What Success Looks Like

  • Month-end close: from 12+ days to 3 days or less, for 80% of customers
  • Warehouse adoption: 90% of frontline staff using the app within 30 days
  • Go-live: under 5 days, at a fixed price
  • Growth: 50 paying customers with 90% retention at 6 months
  • Cost: 35–65% of the 5-year cost of Microsoft Dynamics
Part 2 · Product Architecture

How the product is put together

The event-driven core that links operations and finance.

7 What the System Does

Buying from Suppliers

Create purchase orders, receive deliveries by scanning, capture supplier invoices (read automatically when possible), check invoices against what was ordered and received, and schedule payments.

Selling to Customers

Quotes and orders; pick, pack, and ship with mobile scanning; invoices and payments; returns and credit notes; customer credit limits.

Bookkeeping & Reports

Every operation becomes double-entry bookkeeping automatically. Chart of accounts, profit & loss, balance sheet, cash flow, and a month-end close checklist.

Inventory

Live stock levels across locations, a barcode catalog, transfers and adjustments, and weighted-average valuation.

AI Helpers

Reads supplier invoices, suggests which bills to pay and when, flags duplicates and anomalies, helps map accounts during setup, and classifies unknown barcodes. Always a suggestion — a human decides.

Mobile App for the Warehouse

Scan to receive, pick, transfer, ship, and count stock. Works offline and syncs later. Designed for shared devices and gloves, not desks.

Finance Dashboard

Real-time financial statements, alerts only when something deviates, a month-end close checklist, and one-click trial balance export for accountants.

Still Planned

In the backlog: fast-follow scope such as job and asset events and additional Pit Bay templates — plus multi-entity consolidation polish.

8 How It Works

Everything that happens is recorded as an event in an append-only log: a delivery received, an item picked, an invoice entered. Nothing is edited or deleted — corrections are new events.

Configurable rules translate those events into double-entry bookkeeping. The result is a perfect audit trail, point-in-time reporting, and full traceability: every number can be traced back to who did what, where, and when.

The QuickBooks bridge is optional. It can import opening balances and push records for businesses transitioning off QuickBooks, but the system's own ledger is the source of truth from day one — never a dependency.

Security and multi-tenancy: each customer's data is isolated at the database level, with role-based access and encryption in transit and at rest.

Tech stack: React and TypeScript on the front end; Python with FastAPI and PostgreSQL on the back end — deployed on free-tier hosting for the MVP.

Why it is different: traditional ERPs lose the link between operations and finance. Here, the link is the architecture.

Part 3 · AI in the ERP

Where AI helps — and where it doesn't

A deliberately narrow use of AI: it reduces effort and error in specific, checkable places, and stays out of everything that touches money.

9 AI in the Product

The philosophy: use AI where it clearly helps, and keep a human in the loop for anything that touches money. Augment, don't replace.

Reading supplier invoices. A two-tier approach: a fast path extracts text directly from native PDFs; a vision model handles scanned documents. The system escalates automatically when confidence is low, and anything below the bar goes to a human review queue.

Confidence scoring and human review. Per-field confidence scores highlight exactly what to check. The system learns from corrections but never fully trusts itself.

Matching is deliberately not AI. Checking an invoice against what was ordered and received is rules-based and auditable — financial integrity is never left to a model's guess.

Payment suggestions. Every day the system proposes which bills to pay and which to delay, flags duplicates and outliers, and explains each recommendation in plain language. It never moves money — the controller reviews and approves.

Setup copilot. During onboarding, AI suggests account mappings, finds duplicate customer data and missing items, and classifies unknown barcodes.

Guardrails: no chatbot. No autonomous decisions. AI can be switched off per customer. Every output is a suggestion a person can override.

Part 4 · Technical Implementation

How it was actually built

The method, the quality pipeline, and where the build stands.

10 How It Was Built — BMAD

GrandeSuite Next was built with BMAD — a structured, AI-augmented development method that takes a product from first idea to production in defined phases. Every phase produces documents that feed the next, and named AI agents play different roles: product manager, business analyst, architect, designer, engineer, test architect, writer.

Nothing was skipped: research before requirements, requirements before architecture, architecture before code. Every feature can be traced back to why it exists. In total, the method produced 27 planning documents — one for every area of the system — plus everyday workflows described step by step.

11 Quality & Testing

Tests come first. Acceptance criteria are written before a feature is built, and every criterion maps to at least one automated test.

Three independent reviews per piece of work. A "blind" reviewer sees only the code and hunts for bugs and security issues. An edge-case reviewer walks every branch and boundary. An acceptance reviewer checks the result against the spec.

Readiness checks before building. The first formal assessment found 6 issues — two of them critical — that were fixed before development began. A later assessment in September 2026 surfaced remaining planning gaps, which were addressed or explicitly deferred.

Continuous integration. Every change runs linting, type checks, and the full test suite before merge; deployments are automated.

Quality principle: tests should prove the absence of defects, not just the presence of features.

Part 5 · What I'd Do Differently

What I learned, and what I'd change

The lessons that came out of building it — and the calls I'd make differently next time.

13 Key Learnings

After building this, the things that matter most:

  1. Start with the pain that hurts most. Supplier invoices were the highest-pain, highest-volume process — and the fastest path to visible value. The same architecture supports every other area.
  2. Architect for immutability from day one. The append-only event log made audit, debugging, and point-in-time reporting trivial. Reversing this later would have been nearly impossible.
  3. AI is a tool, not the product. Every AI feature was tested against one question: does it meaningfully reduce human effort or error? Not "is it cool?"
  4. Free-tier AI changes the economics. Running inference on free tiers eliminated the per-invoice costs that make competing tools expensive. A product decision disguised as an infrastructure decision.
  5. The process is the product. The structured pipeline caught alignment issues before they became expensive rework — and made every piece of the system explainable in seconds.
  6. Strategy is a living thing. The project began framed as a financial platform for restaurants. As research matured, the framing moved to something bigger and stronger: an entry-level ERP for growing businesses that have outgrown QuickBooks. Parts of the plan written under the old framing had to be reworked. Settle the market framing before detailed planning — late repositioning is expensive, even when it is the right call.

14 What I'd Do Differently

Bring the testing pipeline in earlier. Formal test architecture came midway through the build; integrating it from the first feature would have caught issues sooner and shortened review cycles.
